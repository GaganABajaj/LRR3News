Changing Our Mindset to Conserve Water in Life Republic

By Community Desk  ·  26 Oct 2026 · 11:59

An opinion on changing our mindset to conserve water in Life Republic

Water is essential to life, yet we often use it without thinking about its value. We open a tap, fill a bucket, wash a car or clean a balcony without considering how much water we consume. As long as water is available, we tend to assume it will always be there.

But with the Government of Maharashtra raising concerns about the possible impact of El Niño on rainfall and the associated risk of water scarcity, it is time to change this attitude.

The government has called for at least a 10% reduction in water consumption by August 31, 2027. The target is clear. The bigger question is: How do we achieve it?

Whenever You Use Water, Believe It Is Expensive

The answer may be simpler than we think.

Whenever you use water, believe that it is expensive.

Imagine that every bucket of water costs ₹100. Would you leave the tap running while brushing your teeth? Would you use fresh water to water trees when STP-treated water could do the job? Would you allow a tank to overflow or ignore a dripping tap?

Probably not.

Our behaviour changes when we recognise the value of something. We handle expensive things carefully, avoid unnecessary waste and think twice before using them carelessly. Water deserves the same respect, even when it flows from a tap at the turn of a handle.

You do not need to calculate the cost of every litre. You simply need to develop the habit of treating water as a precious and expensive resource.

The next time you open a tap, pause for a moment and ask yourself: Would I use this much water if I had to pay ₹100 for every bucket?

That single thought could change the way you use water every day.

Do Not Promise to Save Water. Promise to Respect It.

We often talk about saving water as though it were a special activity requiring extra effort. In reality, water conservation should be part of our everyday behaviour.

Do not make a grand promise to save water. Instead, make a quiet commitment to respect it. The savings will follow naturally.

When brushing your teeth, turn off the tap when you do not need the water. When showering, avoid letting water run unnecessarily. When washing vegetables, use only what you need. If you notice a leaking tap, get it repaired rather than dismissing it as a minor inconvenience.

Before washing a balcony or an outdoor area, ask yourself whether it really needs to be washed at that moment. Before discarding water that could safely be reused, consider whether it could serve another purpose, such as watering plants.

These are not sacrifices. They are sensible, everyday habits.

We already behave this way when water becomes scarce. During a supply disruption, we measure our usage, reuse water where appropriate and avoid unnecessary activities. We suddenly become more careful because we realise how valuable water is.

Why should we wait for a shortage before behaving responsibly?

Rules Cannot Replace Personal Responsibility

In a large township like Life Republic, residents have suggested measures such as restricting the use of fresh water for car washing or introducing water cuts. Such suggestions stem from a genuine concern about water availability.

However, monitoring every household's consumption and enforcing every restriction can be difficult. Even the strictest rules cannot ensure that every tap is turned off when it should be.

Rules may discourage waste, but a change in mindset can prevent it before it happens.

No one needs to watch you while you brush your teeth, wash your car or clean your home. No one needs to monitor your daily water use. The responsibility rests with each of us to act honestly and responsibly, while trusting that others are making a similar effort.

If we use water thoughtfully because we recognise its value, conservation becomes a habit rather than an obligation.

The government has called for a reduction in water consumption of at least 10%. If every resident changes their behaviour, we have a genuine opportunity to meet this target and potentially exceed it without relying entirely on prohibitions or restrictions.

Let Us Make Responsible Behaviour Our Culture

The Cooperative Housing Society (CHS) and the Facilities Management Team can support these efforts by ensuring that recycled water from the sewage treatment plant (STP), used for toilet flushing, is appropriately treated, filtered and maintained, and is available 24 hours a day, seven days a week, wherever the system permits.

A reliable supply of recycled water for flushing can reduce our dependence on fresh water and help us use our available resources more efficiently.

But infrastructure alone will not solve the problem. The real difference will come from how we use water when nobody is watching.

Let us stop thinking of water conservation as something we must do only because the government has issued a directive. Let us begin to see it as a natural expression of our responsibility towards our families, our neighbours and our future.

We cannot control the rainfall, but we can control our habits. We cannot create more water simply by turning on a tap, but we can make the water available to us go further.

So, whenever you use water, believe that it is expensive. Use only what you need, avoid waste and respect every drop.

If thousands of residents adopt this simple belief, the collective impact could be far greater than that of rules imposed from outside.

Let us make water conservation a habit, not a campaign; a mindset, not a mandate.

Because when we truly value water, saving it becomes second nature.
