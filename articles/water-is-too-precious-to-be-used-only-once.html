Water Is Too Precious to Be Used Only Once

By Aditya Bhadalkar  ·  15 Nov 2026 · 16:12

Are housing societies ready to walk the talk on water conservation and reuse?

Every year, particularly before and during summer, water conservation becomes a familiar topic of discussion. We hear about water shortages, falling groundwater levels, tanker dependency, rainwater harvesting and the need to use water responsibly. Residents are advised to conserve water, societies are encouraged to adopt sustainable practices, and authorities discuss long-term water security.

But beyond the discussions, how many of us are actually preparing for a future where water availability can no longer be taken for granted?

More importantly, are our housing societies doing enough to reduce their dependence on external water supplies, or are we still following the traditional approach of using freshwater once, treating the resulting wastewater, and then letting much of that resource go underutilised?

Perhaps it is time to ask a different question: Why should water be treated as waste after its first use when technology can potentially recover it and put it back to use?

The water situation is becoming harder to ignore

Pune's recent experience is a reminder that even established urban water supply systems can face pressure. In October 2026, following concerns over rainfall deficits and reservoir levels, the Pune Municipal Corporation introduced a 10% water cut accompanied by weekly supply shutdowns. The possibility of deeper restrictions was also discussed. Some planned shutdowns were temporarily cancelled around Diwali, but the underlying concerns about water allocation and future availability remained.

This is not merely a municipal supply issue. Large residential townships and housing societies often depend on a centralised source of water. Any reduction, delay or disruption in that supply can affect thousands of households.

The bigger picture is equally concerning.

According to India's Economic Survey 2025–26, India generates an estimated 112 billion litres of domestic and industrial wastewater every day. The Survey reports that only 28% is treated and that cities recycle only 8% of their treated wastewater.

Think about the opportunity being missed.

We continue to search for additional freshwater sources while a substantial quantity of wastewater, which could potentially become a valuable resource, remains underutilised.

[Reference: Economic Survey 2025–26 – Chapter 15, Urbanisation]

From talking about water conservation to actually practising it

Water conservation should not be limited to awareness campaigns, posters, occasional appeals to residents or discussions during society meetings.

It should become part of our infrastructure planning.

Most large housing societies already have sewage treatment plants (STPs). These plants treat wastewater generated by residents, and the treated water is generally reused for toilet flushing, gardening and landscaping.

That is a good beginning. However, it raises another question:

Are we making the maximum possible use of the water that we have already treated?

What if the water coming out of an existing STP could undergo further purification, allowing it to be reused for additional purposes? What if the same water could be recovered through multiple treatment and reuse cycles rather than being treated as a disposable resource?

This is where an interesting technology offered by a company called BOSON WHITE WATER caught my attention while casually browsing online.

What is BOSON WHITE WATER offering?

BOSON WHITE WATER, a water-treatment and recycling company, offers a system designed to process wastewater that has already undergone treatment in an existing STP.

The company's offering is based on an advanced, multi-stage tertiary treatment system. Rather than replacing the conventional STP, the system is installed downstream of it to further improve the quality of the treated water.

According to the company's published information, its BOSON White Water system uses an 11-stage treatment process involving several filtration and purification stages, membrane-based treatment and UV disinfection.

The broad idea is to remove remaining suspended particles, turbidity, organic contaminants, dissolved impurities and microorganisms from the water coming out of the STP.

The company also describes an IoT-enabled monitoring system intended to track water quality and plant performance.

In simple terms, the objective is to take water that has already undergone conventional sewage treatment and recover a higher-quality water resource from it.

The company currently advertises an operational expenditure (OPEX) model, under which it installs, operates and maintains its treatment equipment and charges customers based on water supplied. This is worth investigating, although the actual commercial terms would need to be evaluated for each installation.

How can it work alongside an existing STP?

This is perhaps the most interesting aspect for residential societies.

An existing STP does not necessarily have to be dismantled or replaced to explore advanced wastewater recovery.

A simplified treatment arrangement would look like this:

Raw Sewage Generated by Residents

↓

Existing Sewage Treatment Plant (STP)

Primary and biological/secondary sewage treatment

↓

Treated Wastewater

↓

BOSON Tertiary Treatment System

Additional filtration, purification, membrane treatment and disinfection

↓

Recovered Higher-Quality Water

↓

Further Reuse for Suitable Applications

The existing STP continues to perform its primary responsibility of treating sewage. The additional system further processes the treated output.

This approach could be particularly relevant for societies that already have an operational STP but want to improve the quality and usefulness of its treated water.

However, one distinction is important: tertiary treatment cannot automatically compensate for an overloaded, malfunctioning or poorly maintained STP. The incoming water quality must be suitable for the additional treatment system, and both plants must operate reliably.

Is there evidence of actual installations?

There are published examples worth examining.

One example is Emmanuel Heights Apartment in Bengaluru. A case study published by the Solar Impulse Foundation describes an 11-stage BOSON system installed downstream of the apartment's existing STP. It reports recovery of up to 60,000 litres per day, or approximately 21 million litres annually, with the recovered water being supplied to industries. The case study also reports savings of approximately ₹80,000 per month in wastewater disposal costs.

These are published project figures, not guaranteed savings for another housing society. The case study itself notes that the Foundation does not independently guarantee the completeness or accuracy of the information.

Read the Emmanuel Heights case study

Another interesting example is the Devanahalli water-reuse project in Karnataka, developed by Biome Environmental Trust in collaboration with BOSON.

This project follows an indirect potable reuse approach. Treated wastewater is introduced into lakes, blended with rainwater, and allowed to recharge an aquifer through natural filtration. The recovered water undergoes further treatment before being supplied to residents.

WELL Labs documents a current supply of approximately 6.4 lakh litres per day, serving more than 45,000 residents.

Read the Devanahalli project details – WELL Labs

These examples demonstrate that wastewater recovery is not merely a theoretical concept. However, different applications require different treatment processes and safeguards.

Can the same water be recycled multiple times?

This deserves particular attention.

There is a common misconception that once water has been used and treated, it can only be reused once or twice before it becomes unsuitable.

There is no universal limit on the number of times a water molecule can be recycled.

Water is not consumed in the same way as fuel. It moves through a continuous cycle of use, wastewater generation, treatment and reuse.

For example:

  1. Water is supplied to households and used for domestic purposes.
  2. The resulting wastewater enters the STP.
  3. The STP treats the wastewater.
  4. Further purification produces water suitable for another intended application.
  5. That water is reused.
  6. Where the subsequent use generates wastewater that returns to the sewer system, it can undergo treatment again.

This cycle can continue repeatedly.

However, this does not mean that 100% of the water can be recovered indefinitely. Some water is lost through evaporation, landscaping and other uses. Sludge and concentrated reject streams also need to be managed. Dissolved salts and other contaminants must be removed or controlled to prevent their accumulation.

Advanced treatment systems can help recover a larger proportion of water, but their actual recovery rate, reject quantity and operating requirements must be established through technical data.

The important takeaway is that there is no predetermined maximum number of reuse cycles. The objective should be to recover and reuse as much water as is technically, economically and environmentally practical.

Why should housing societies investigate this?

For a large residential society, the potential benefits extend beyond simply improving the appearance of flushing water.

1. Reduced dependence on external water supplies

By recovering more treated wastewater, societies may be able to reduce the quantity of freshwater required for applications such as flushing, gardening, landscaping and common-area cleaning.

2. Better treated-water quality

Additional treatment may improve colour, odour, suspended particles, hardness and other quality parameters, depending on the technology and the characteristics of the incoming STP water.

3. Better utilisation of existing infrastructure

Instead of immediately considering a complete STP replacement, societies could investigate whether their existing plants can be upgraded through additional treatment.

4. Reduced wastewater discharge

Recovering water for productive use can reduce the quantity of treated wastewater that needs to be discharged or transported elsewhere.

5. Potential operational benefits

A professionally operated additional treatment system, particularly under a suitable OPEX arrangement, could reduce the maintenance burden associated with that equipment. However, actual savings must be compared against the cost per kilolitre of recovered water, electricity, plumbing modifications and other expenses.

6. Improved long-term water resilience

The ultimate benefit could be a more diversified water supply, where societies depend less on a single external source and make greater use of the wastewater they generate themselves.

Should societies explore potable water reuse too?

This is an even bigger possibility.

If appropriately designed treatment processes can produce water meeting drinking-water quality requirements, why should potable reuse be ruled out altogether?

However, this must be approached with extreme care.

Producing a sample that meets the Bureau of Indian Standards' IS 10500 drinking-water requirements is not, by itself, sufficient to establish that an entire wastewater-to-tap system is safe.

Potable reuse requires validated treatment barriers, source-water controls, continuous quality monitoring, water safety management, appropriate approvals and safeguards throughout storage and distribution.

The Devanahalli project is an example of indirect potable reuse, not simply a tertiary treatment unit connected directly to apartment drinking-water tanks.

The World Health Organization's guidance on potable reuse provides a useful explanation of the safety requirements.

WHO – Potable Reuse: Guidance for Producing Safe Drinking-Water

For residential societies, non-potable reuse remains a practical starting point. Potable reuse can be a longer-term subject for technical and regulatory investigation.

What should societies do next?

Rather than immediately committing to a particular company or technology, housing societies should begin by understanding their own water balance.

Some basic questions deserve attention:

  • How much sewage does the STP receive and how much treated water does it produce daily?
  • How much treated water is actually reused?
  • How much is discharged or remains unused?
  • What is the existing treated-water quality, based on independent laboratory reports?
  • Can additional treatment improve the output sufficiently for more applications?
  • What percentage of water can realistically be recovered?
  • What happens to the rejected or concentrated water?
  • What would be the actual cost per kilolitre, including power, operation and maintenance?
  • Can the existing plumbing, tanks and pumps accommodate additional reuse?

Societies should also compare technologies and obtain performance guarantees supported by independent laboratory testing.

The Central Pollution Control Board has published guidelines for the reuse of treated sewage water, including recommended quality parameters for different applications. This is a useful starting point for understanding why water intended for toilet flushing, landscaping and other uses may require different quality specifications.

CPCB – Guidelines for Reuse of Treated Sewage Water

The question is not whether we can afford to waste water

The question is whether we can afford to continue doing so.

We spend considerable amounts on developing and maintaining water infrastructure, yet wastewater recovery often receives less attention than obtaining additional freshwater supplies.

Every large housing society with an STP should at least investigate whether its treated wastewater can be further purified and reused. We do not necessarily have to adopt BOSON or any particular vendor. What matters is understanding the available technology, evaluating its feasibility and taking informed decisions.

Perhaps the time has come to move beyond simply treating sewage to meet minimum requirements and start treating wastewater as a resource that deserves to be recovered, reused and managed responsibly.

We may not be able to control rainfall, reservoir levels or the decisions of external water suppliers. But we can take greater responsibility for how efficiently we use the water already available to us.

Water conservation is not just about using less water. It is also about making better use of every litre we receive.
